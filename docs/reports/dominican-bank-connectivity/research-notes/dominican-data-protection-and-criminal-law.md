# Dominican data protection and adjacent rules for user-authorized bank data (U2b)

Research date: 2026-09-27. Access date for every source below: 2026-09-27 unless a row says otherwise.

This is legal research, not a legal opinion. It maps public Dominican rules that bear on a non-bank service (Argus, hosted on US cloud services) that receives, stores, and processes individuals' bank balances and transactions with their consent. Nothing here says a practice is lawful or unlawful. Rows marked "Inference" are the researcher's reading and need Dominican counsel.

Status legend: "Enacted and effective", "Enacted, not yet effective", "Bill" (with chamber status), "Announced". Guidance pages are marked "Official guidance".

Source labels. Primary sources are the Gaceta Oficial (read through a FAOLEX copy for the Constitution), official PDFs on presidencia.gob.do, sb.gob.do, proconsumidor.gob.do, and poderjudicial.gob.do, the Chamber of Deputies' legislative tracker (diputadosrd.gob.do/sil) and its document service, the Senate's archive (memoriahistorica.senadord.gob.do) and news pages, and a Consultoría Jurídica letter. Rows that rely on press, law-firm notes, or a non-official host say "secondary". Ley 53-07 and Ley 126-02 were read on a secondary host because no official copy would open.

Headline findings. Ley 172-13 is still the only general data protection law, with no supervisory authority outside credit bureaus and no breach-notification duty. Two 2026 data protection bills sit in a Chamber commission with expired deadlines. A new Penal Code (Ley 74-25, in force August 2026, amended by Ley 44-26) adds crimes for identity theft that name usernames and passwords, and for collecting or keeping personal data by automated means without consent or after consent is withdrawn. Ley 53-07's unlawful-access offenses appear to survive beside it. No data-localization rule was found.

## 2. Narrative notes

### T1. Constitution, arts. 44 and 70

The brief named the 2010 Constitution as amended in 2015. A further reform was proclaimed on 27 Oct 2024 and published in G.O. No. 11170 on 31 Oct 2024. The Senate's account of that reform lists the amended articles (81, 166, 167, 169, 171, 178, 179, 209, 268, 274, plus a new 278). Arts. 44, 53, 70, and 109 are not on the list, so they carry over unchanged (inference). The texts quoted in the matrix come from G.O. 11170.

Art. 44.2 is the constitutional base for data protection. It gives a right of access to data held in official or private registers, sets five processing principles (quality, lawfulness, loyalty, security, purpose), and sends disputes to the courts. The opening paragraph of art. 44 makes private parties, not only authorities, liable to repair privacy harms. Art. 44.3 protects private documents and messages in digital form and electronic communications, and names only judicial orders as the way to open them. Art. 70 (hábeas data) is the judicial action to see and correct one's data. No constitutional text creates a data protection agency. Enforcement is judicial.

### T2. Ley 172-13

Scope is the main uncertainty. The text reads broadly in places. Art. 2 applies the law to personal data in any database and any later use, public or private; art. 41 says private parties who build databases not for purely personal use must meet the law; art. 13 lists duties for every *responsable del tratamiento*. Other parts read narrowly. Arts. 1 and 19 speak of databases "destinados a dar informes" (meant to provide reports), and a large share of the articles (29 to 36, 43, 47 to 69, 81 to 83) regulate credit bureaus (Sociedades de Información Crediticia). Secondary commentary (IPANDETEC 2022, ECIJA 2024) describes the law as mainly a credit-bureau statute. Inference: the principles, consent rules, rights, security and secrecy duties, and criminal provisions are drafted to reach every controller. The supervisory authority and the administrative sanctions reach only credit bureaus. Against anyone else, enforcement runs through hábeas data, civil damages (art. 16), and the criminal articles (84, 86 to 88).

Consent must be free, express, and conscious, in writing or an equivalent medium, and prominent when bundled with other terms, after notice of purpose, recipients, controller identity and address, and rights (arts. 5.3, 5.4, 6.7). Art. 27 lists exceptions, including data needed to perform a contract with the person (27.4). Disclosure to others needs a legitimate interest on both sides and prior consent of at least one data subject (art. 28). Financial data are not "sensitive", but transaction lines can reveal sensitive facts indirectly, which arts. 75 and 76 address with express written consent. International transfers need consent or another listed ground such as contract performance (art. 80). There is no adequacy test and no localization rule. Access requests get 5 business days; rectification and deletion get 10, with notice to recipients within 5 (arts. 8, 10, 12). Cancellation means blocking during the liability limitation period, then deletion (art. 15). Security and secrecy duties bind the controller and any processor (arts. 5.5, 5.6, 13, 42). The law has no breach-notification duty and no processor-contract rule.

Two provisions deserve counsel's attention for an aggregator. Art. 30 reserves to Junta Monetaria-authorized credit bureaus the service of collecting, processing, and exchanging credit-history information that comes from financial intermediaries. Whether a consumer app that holds a user's own loan and card data could be caught by that wording is Unknown. Art. 88 punishes any natural or legal person who violates the law with 6 months to 2 years of prison and 100 to 150 minimum wages, which gives every duty in the law a criminal edge.

Bill 05407-2024-2028-CD (Deputy Charles Noel Mariotti Paz, PLD) was deposited 17 Mar 2026. Bill 05670-2024-2028-CD (Deputy Vicente Arturo Sánchez Henríquez, PRM) was deposited 30 Apr 2026. ### T4. Ley 53-07 and the new Penal Code

Two layers now apply. Ley 53-07 (2007) is a special law that stays in force except where it contradicts the new Penal Code, Ley 74-25 (promulgated 3 Aug 2025, in force from early August 2026). Ley 74-25 art. 391.7 repeals only the "contrary" parts of Ley 53-07 and art. 392 keeps special laws for conduct the code does not cover, so which Ley 53-07 articles survive is not stated. The new code has no general unauthorized-access offense (none found in its article titles), which suggests (inference) that Ley 53-07 arts. 5 and 6 remain the access offenses. Ley 44-26 (27 Jul 2026) then amended many Penal Code articles, including arts. 188 to 190 (identity) and 391 to 393, and deferred the start of corporate criminal liability (arts. 8 to 11) by three months from 5 Aug 2026, per Diario Libre (secondary). The official text of Ley 44-26 was not read.

Authorization is framed in different ways. Ley 53-07 defines unlawful access as entering a system without authorization, or "a través del acceso de un tercero" (art. 4), punishes access with or without another's identity, or by exceeding an authorization (art. 6), punishes capturing or using access codes through which unlawful access is obtained (art. 5), and punishes privacy-invading devices used without legitimate cause or authorization from the competent entity (art. 19). No article says who can give the authorization for a bank's system, the bank or the account holder. Ley 74-25 frames the data offense around the data subject's prior consent and its withdrawal (art. 198), frames identity theft around using an assumed or another's identity, with usernames and passwords listed as identifying information (art. 188), and requires intent for interception of electronic correspondence (art. 197). Ley 53-07 reaches conduct from abroad with effects in the Dominican Republic (art. 2); the Penal Code's territorial rules were not reviewed.

The questions for counsel, stated without an answer, are these. For a third party that logs in to a bank with the customer's credentials and consent: does the customer's consent count as "autorización" when the bank's terms forbid sharing credentials; does the "a través del acceso de un tercero" wording capture the pattern; is it use of "another's identity" under Ley 53-07 art. 17 and Penal Code art. 188 as amended; and would the service be treated as a custodian of identifying information for the aggravated tier of art. 188. For software that runs inside the customer's own authenticated session: is that the customer's own access with a tool; does automated reading "exceed an authorization" if the bank forbids automation; and do Ley 53-07 art. 19 or Penal Code art. 197 párrafo I apply to a program that reads the customer's own pages or statements with consent.

No replacement cybercrime law was enacted. Bills to amend Ley 53-07 lapsed (03736 and 04857 in the Chamber, 01605-2013 in the Senate). The Executive's 2022 bill to replace Ley 53-07 was not found in the current tracker (inference: lapsed). Two cybersecurity bills are alive in the Chamber. Bill 05458 ("gestión de la ciberseguridad") was declared urgent and approved in first reading on 24 Jul 2026 and returned to commission for second discussion. Bill 06132 ("sistema nacional de la ciberseguridad contra la ciberdelincuencia") went to commission on 2 Sep 2026. Their texts were not read.

### T5. Ley 126-02, electronic consent and records

Ley 126-02 gives data messages legal effect (art. 4), treats an accessible data message as meeting a legal "writing" requirement (art. 5), and accepts a signature requirement only through a digital signature that meets the law (art. 6). Digital records carry the evidentiary force of private signed documents, weighed by how reliably they were created, stored, and attributed (arts. 9, 10). Contracts and declarations of will may be formed by data message (arts. 13, 14). The open point for Argus is how Ley 172-13's *por escrito* consent maps onto this. Inference: if counsel reads it as a writing requirement, an in-app consent record that stays accessible should qualify under art. 5; if counsel reads it as needing a signature, art. 6 points to certificate-based digital signatures. The same question arises for a customer's express authorization to a bank under Ley 183-02 art. 56(b). The Chamber tracker shows no pending bill to amend Ley 126-02 (keyword searches for "126-02", "comercio electrónico", "firma digital", "firmas electrónicas" returned nothing).

### T6. Ley 358-05 and financial consumers

Ley 358-05 is the general consumer law. It is public order but supplementary to sectoral laws (art. 2). In sectoral matters the consumer uses the sectoral procedure, the more favorable rule wins a conflict, and in doubt Ley 358-05 prevails (art. 135). Bank customers' complaints against banks go to the Superintendencia de Bancos' ProUsuario office under the Junta Monetaria's user-protection regulation, whose scope is banks and exchange and remittance agents only (art. 3). Inference: ProUsuario's rules do not bind Argus directly. Pro Consumidor could treat Argus as a "proveedor" (the law covers foreign providers), but the consumer definition requires acquiring goods or services "a título oneroso", so a free tier may fall outside it (inference). No data protection provision appears in Ley 358-05's text, so the secondary claim that Pro Consumidor oversees data protection in consumer matters has no basis found in the law. Bank-side rules still matter. Ley 183-02 art. 56(b) lets a bank release a client's data only to the holder or to a person the holder expressly authorizes by reliable legal means, and the user-protection regulation gives users a right to the information banks generate about them (art. 6.k).

### T7. Cross-border processing, cloud, retention, deletion, localization, breach notice

No localization requirement was found for a non-bank. Ley 172-13 art. 80 allows transfers abroad on consent or contract necessity, and its definition of international transfer covers processing on behalf of a controller established in the Dominican Republic. Whether a foreign company that collects directly from Dominican users is an "exporter" (defined as located in Dominican territory) is Unknown. For banks, the Junta Monetaria's 2018 cybersecurity regulation requires a documented cloud policy and security terms in outsourcing contracts (art. 36) but no local hosting (text searched). The same regulation may extend to "support and related-service entities" linked to banks electronically (art. 3 párrafo), which could matter if Argus connects to a bank by API. Retention and deletion rules are indirect. They are blocking then deletion (Ley 172-13 art. 15), a criminal bar on keeping data after consent is withdrawn (Ley 74-25 art. 198), 90-day log retention for *proveedores de servicio* (Ley 53-07 art. 56, term undefined), and format conditions for digital records (Ley 126-02 art. 11). No enacted breach-notification duty for a non-bank was found. The pending cybersecurity bill 05458 might add one; its text was not read.

### T8. Official guidance on credential sharing and delegated or automated access

ProUsuario, the SB's consumer office, tells users never to share passwords with anyone, to use only official channels and app stores (6 Mar 2023), and not to lend accounts or cards (7 Dec 2022). This is guidance, not regulation, but it is the regulator's public message and runs against a model that asks users for bank passwords. The 2018 cybersecurity regulation requires banks to inventory and protect client connections to electronic channels (art. 23), so banks are expected to monitor how customers connect (inference: third-party logins may be flagged). No SB, ProUsuario, or Banco Central guidance was found on aggregators, delegated access, or automated access. The SB's innovation page announces open-banking regulation for the second half of 2026 with no draft or consultation shown.
